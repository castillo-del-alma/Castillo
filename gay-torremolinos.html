The history↓
Why this town

A free space in the middle of a dictatorship

Most people know Torremolinos as a beach town of tower blocks and English breakfasts. In the 1960s it was something else entirely: the one place in Spain where you could be openly gay without hiding — while the rest of the country lived under Franco.

It lasted nine years. Then it closed in a single night.

The story is not well known outside Spain, and it deserves better. It also explains why Torremolinos is still the gay town of this coastline — and why a couple of streets here matter to a lot of people.

1962–1971

Pasaje Begoña

Pasaje Begoña is a small L-shaped alley in the middle of Torremolinos. In the closing months of 1962 the first bars opened there where gay people could gather freely — the first of their kind anywhere in Spain.

In under a decade it grew to at least fifty bars and live music venues in that single alley: flamenco, jazz, rock and folk side by side. Tourists came from across Europe and the United States. Hollywood stars, writers and European royalty walked the same streets.

That it was possible at all had nothing to do with tolerance. It had to do with foreign currency. Tourism brought money into a poor country, and the regime wanted to show the world a modern Spain. As long as it stayed inside Torremolinos, the authorities looked the other way.

24 June 1971

The night it all closed

On the night of San Juan in 1971, police raided Pasaje Begoña. It ended nine years of freedom, and the aftermath lasted for decades.

More than 300 people

The raid caught over three hundred people in a single night. It was not one bar, but the whole alley.

114 arrested

Those arrested were taken to Málaga. Witnesses remember them in the courtyard of the Palacio de la Aduana — today the city museum.

Deported

Dozens of foreigners were expelled from Spain. Freedom had drawn them there; now it was the reason they were sent home.

Files and surveillance

Everyone identified had a police file opened on them and was told they were being watched. It followed people for years.

The law behind it

The basis was the Ley de Peligrosidad Social, which had replaced the old vagrancy law and turned homosexuality into a question of 'social danger'.

The bars never came back

Many venues were closed for good. Part of the scene went back into hiding, and the town's golden tourist years took a blow at the same time.

The aftermath

From oblivion to recognition

For decades the story stood still. The alley was renamed in the 1980s, the bars were gone, and while Stonewall became a global reference, Pasaje Begoña quietly fell into disrepair.

That changed when a local association began digging through archives and tracking down the people who had been there. Today the site is officially recognised as a place of historical memory and as the birthplace of LGBTI rights in Spain.

The 1980s

The alley is renamed Pasaje Gil Vicente. The name Begoña disappears from the map — and the history with it.

2018

Asociación Pasaje Begoña is founded and begins gathering testimony and archive material.

2019

During WorldPride in New York the association forms a twinning with the Stonewall Inn Foundation.

2021

Another twinning follows, this time with the onePULSE Foundation in Orlando.

The recognition

The Spanish Congress and the Andalusian Parliament recognise Pasaje Begoña as a place of historical memory and the birthplace of LGBTI rights in Spain.

Small things from back then

Five details that stay with you

Stories from a town where, for nine years, anything was possible.

Lennon on the terrace

Witnesses from the time recall John Lennon sitting on one of the alley's terraces with Beatles manager Brian Epstein, watching people go by. The story is oral history — but it is told by people who were there.

The name

Pasaje Begoña is named after the daughter of the man who built the block. No political gesture — just a father and his daughter.

Fifty bars in one alley

It is an alley you can walk through in half a minute. In nine years it held at least fifty venues.

The four S's

The tourism of the era was sold on sun, sea, sand and sex. The last S was the reason some people dared to travel here at all.

Spain's Stonewall

The Spanish press often calls the site Spain's Stonewall. Chronologically Torremolinos came first: the bars opened in 1962, seven years before the New York riots.

The town today

La Nogalera and the coast

The scene is still in Torremolinos, a couple of hundred metres from where it began. Everything sits close together: bars, beach and promenade are a fifteen-minute walk apart.

La Nogalera

The heart of the scene. An open shopping complex in the middle of town where bars and clubs are packed into a square around the plaza. Most of the nightlife happens here.

The second area

Across the road, around Calle Danza Invisible and Calle Casablanca, sits a smaller cluster of venues. A few minutes' walk from La Nogalera.

The beach

There are two gay beach clubs on the shore, both walkable from the centre. Sunbeds, food and music all day — this is where the afternoon goes.

The promenade

The Paseo Marítimo runs along the water from Bajondillo to the old fishing quarter of La Carihuela, where the classic beach restaurants are. Walk it at sunset.

Calle San Miguel

The town's pedestrian street, five minutes from La Nogalera. Shops, ice cream and people watching people — the daytime opposite of the evening.

Pride and the summer festivals

Torremolinos Pride fills the town, and several large gay festivals follow across the summer. The dates move from year to year — check the official programme before booking flights.

Before you go

Getting there

A rental car is easiest if you are combining coast and estate. For Torremolinos alone, the coastal train (Cercanías C-1) runs from Málaga via the airport into town.

When to go

June to September is high season: hot and busy. May and October still give you swimming weather with half the crowds — and markedly cheaper flights.

The rhythm of the day

The town wakes late. Dinner starts around 9pm and the bars fill after midnight. Plan around a northern European supper and you will have the room to yourself.

Language

Spanish is the main language, but English covers almost everything in the tourist area. A few words of Spanish are noticed and appreciated.

Pasaje Begoña today

The alley is in the centre, minutes from La Nogalera. There are memorial plaques, but it is not a museum — allow a quarter of an hour, not a morning.
